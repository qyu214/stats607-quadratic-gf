# Project 2 — Critical Analysis

For this project, I used a quadratic teacher–student network to study how the covariance spectrum and student width affect learning under population gradient flow. The setup comes from my research, but unlike the original paper's online-SGD simulations, I worked directly with population dynamics. I was mainly interested in separating two factors behind the final risk: the approximation error due to limited model capacity and the remaining gap above that limit at a given training time.

I started with $d=32$, varying $\beta\in\{0.1,0.35,0.6\}$ and student width $r_s\in\{8,16\}$, with ten random initializations per condition. I kept $\alpha+\beta=0.7$, which makes the theoretical rank floor independent of $\beta$ for a fixed width. This allowed me to compare learning trajectories without changing the best achievable risk. At $t=80$, the floors were about 0.1774 for width 8 and 0.0779 for width 16. The gap between the actual risk and the floor increased with $\beta$ for both widths. The wider network had lower absolute risk, but it was not necessarily closer to its own optimum at the same time.

I then added two follow-up experiments. First, at $d=256$ with $\alpha=\beta=0.35$, I compared widths 32, 64, and 128 using five initializations each. At $t=160$, the mean risks decreased from 0.1243 to 0.0738 and 0.0377, while the corresponding gaps increased from 0.0010 to 0.0031 and 0.0071. This is why absolute risk alone can be misleading: a wider network may achieve lower risk while remaining farther above its own rank-constrained optimum.

The main experiment changed $\alpha$ whenever $\beta$ changed, because their sum was fixed. To see how much the results depended on that choice, I also fixed $\alpha=0.35$ and varied $\beta$ over 0.25, 0.35, and 0.55 at $d=32$ and width 16. The rank floors decreased as $\beta$ increased, while the gaps at $t=80$ increased slightly. The differences were modest relative to Monte Carlo uncertainty, so I treat this as a sensitivity check rather than a general trend. The $\alpha=\beta=0.35$ condition also repeats the main experiment's baseline, rather than adding an independent set of runs.

I kept the same risk definition, numerical solver settings, and bias-update timescale across experiments. I also reused initializations across $\beta$ values for a given dimension and width. The main implementation challenges were getting the risk normalization and gradient-flow time scale right, keeping the trainable bias in the dynamics, and making sure the fixed-$\alpha$ plots used a different rank floor for each $\beta$. I checked the gradients numerically and included tests for the rank floor and reproducibility.

The model is idealized: the features are Gaussian, the teacher and covariance are aligned and diagonal, there is no observation noise, and training uses exact population expectations rather than finite samples. Each follow-up uses only five or ten initializations, and the Monte Carlo intervals do not include numerical integration error. Fixing $\alpha$ also does not keep the normalized teacher completely unchanged, since $\|T\Sigma\|_F$ depends on $\beta$. If I continued this study, I would check solver-tolerance sensitivity and then compare with finite-sample SGD. Overall, these simulations suggest that lower risk and smaller distance from the rank-constrained optimum are not the same thing.
